how did they get in?

july 2026

we have weekly paper discussions in our group. we create a poll of papers, vote on which ones to read, and then discuss them together. we are a machine learning group, therefore we often talk about ml conference papers. most of the researchers working on pde emulation know that there are still not established benchmarks and baseline models (there are some recent efforts in this direction for example apebench, pdebench are some of them). another issue is that scientists tend to compare with weaker numerical baselines as shown in this paper. so a good chunk of the ml conference papers, apart from being flashy, often lack rigorous evaluation like papers published in other venues. after we make the final remarks, i almost always find myself asking it out loud "how did they get in?" there can be many different explanations for this. however, recently being on the other side of the review process, i have a better understanding of how papers end up in ml conferences. the trouble starts even before the review process begins.

bidding process and reviewer assignment

the total number of abstracts sent to me for bidding was close to 400 if i remember correctly. each reviewer had to bid on the papers they were interested in reviewing, indicating their level of expertise and willingness to review them. based on these bids, the program committee assigned reviewers to papers, trying to match expertise with the content of the submissions. i am aware that they do rank the abstracts according to their relevance for the reviewers. but they still require reviewers to bid for at least 30 papers with a high level of interest. this indirectly means that one needs to read a significant number of abstracts to make sure that they balance their expertise and interests (i tried to read all the abstracts, i think eventually did).
